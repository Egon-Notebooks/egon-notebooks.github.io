# Self-compassion

Treating yourself with the same kindness you would offer a close friend can feel surprisingly difficult. Self-compassion — the practice of responding to your own struggles, mistakes, and pain with warmth rather than harsh judgment — is something many people have to actively learn and practice. Research suggests it can play a meaningful role in emotional wellbeing, and the good news is that it is a skill that can grow over time.

One place to start is noticing the inner voice that speaks up when things go wrong. Many people find that this voice is far more critical toward themselves than it would ever be toward someone they care about. Simply becoming aware of that tone — without immediately trying to change it — can be a first step. Some people find it helpful to ask themselves: "What would I say to a friend in this situation?" That gentle shift in perspective can open up a little more space for kindness.

Another approach that many people find useful is acknowledging difficulty without exaggerating it or pushing it away. This is sometimes called mindful self-compassion — recognizing that something feels hard, that pain and setbacks are part of shared human experience, and that you deserve care in those moments just as much as anyone else does. Brief practices like placing a hand on your chest, taking a slow breath, and offering yourself a simple phrase of comfort may feel awkward at first but tend to become more natural with time.

Journaling is another strategy worth exploring. Writing about a difficult experience with curiosity and kindness, rather than self-blame, can help shift the relationship you have with your own story.

Building self-compassion is a gradual process, and many people find it meaningful work to do alongside a therapist or counselor. Support is available, and you do not have to figure it all out on your own.
