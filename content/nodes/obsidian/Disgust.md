# Disgust

Disgust is one of the most universal human emotions, yet it rarely gets the same attention as [[Sadness|sadness]] or [[Understanding anxiety|anxiety]]. It can arise in response to things in our environment, but it can also turn inward — becoming directed at ourselves, our bodies, our thoughts, or our past actions. Understanding this emotion and how it works can be an important step toward relating to it in a healthier way.

One thing many people find helpful is simply recognizing disgust as a normal part of emotional life. Like all emotions, it carries information — it evolved partly to protect us from harm. However, when disgust becomes chronic or self-directed, it can begin to interfere with [[Self-compassion|self-compassion]] and wellbeing. Noticing when this happens, without judgment, is a meaningful first step. Some people find it useful to pause and ask: "What is this feeling trying to tell me, and is that message accurate or fair?"

Practices rooted in self-compassion — such as mindfulness, reflective journaling, or speaking kindly to oneself as you might to a trusted friend — can help soften the grip of self-directed disgust over time. Research suggests that self-compassion can reduce [[Shame and guilt|shame]] and self-criticism, which often travel alongside this emotion. It is also worth noting that disgust, particularly when it is intense or persistent, can sometimes be connected to experiences of [[Understanding trauma|trauma]], OCD, or [[Body image and self-acceptance|body image]] concerns. In these cases, working with a mental health professional can make a real difference.

It is entirely possible to develop a more balanced relationship with difficult emotions like disgust. Many people have found that, with time and the right support, emotions that once felt overwhelming become more manageable. If disgust — whether directed outward or inward — is affecting your daily life or sense of self-worth, reaching out to a professional is a compassionate and courageous step.
