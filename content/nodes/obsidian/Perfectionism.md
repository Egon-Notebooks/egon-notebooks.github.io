# Perfectionism

Perfectionism is something many people recognize in themselves — the persistent drive to meet impossibly high standards, and the sharp self-criticism that follows when those standards aren't met. While having goals and caring about quality can be healthy, perfectionism often goes further, linking a person's sense of worth to their performance. Over time, this pattern can contribute to [[Understanding anxiety|anxiety]], burnout, procrastination, and low [[Self-esteem and where it comes from|self-esteem]].

One strategy that many people find helpful is learning to notice the difference between striving for excellence and demanding perfection. Excellence allows for effort, growth, and occasional mistakes. Perfectionism, by contrast, tends to treat anything less than flawless as a failure. Practicing [[Self-compassion|self-compassion]] — speaking to yourself with the same kindness you might offer a friend — can gently interrupt this cycle. Research consistently supports self-compassion as a tool for reducing self-criticism without reducing motivation or care.

Another approach involves challenging [[All-or-nothing thinking|all-or-nothing thinking]], which is a common pattern in perfectionism. This might mean asking questions like, "Is there a middle ground here?" or "What would 'good enough' actually look like?" [[Cognitive behavioral therapy]] (CBT) — a talking therapy that helps people examine and shift unhelpful thought patterns — offers structured techniques for working through this kind of thinking. Many people find that even small shifts in how they interpret mistakes can make a meaningful difference over time.

It can also help to reflect on where perfectionism may have developed. For some people, it grew out of early experiences where approval felt conditional on performance. Understanding these roots, with the help of a supportive professional, can be an important part of loosening perfectionism's grip.

If perfectionism is affecting your quality of life, relationships, or wellbeing, support is available. Reaching out to a mental health professional is a meaningful step — and one that many people find makes a real difference.
