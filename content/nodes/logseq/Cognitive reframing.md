- Cognitive reframing is a technique rooted in cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) that involves shifting the way we interpret situations, thoughts, or feelings. Rather than changing the circumstances themselves, it's about finding a different — and often more balanced — perspective on them. Many people find this approach helpful for managing [stress]([[Understanding stress]]), [anxiety]([[Understanding anxiety]]), and [low mood]([[Understanding low mood]]).
- One foundational aspect of cognitive reframing is learning to notice unhelpful thought patterns. These are sometimes called "cognitive distortions" — ways of thinking that can feel very real but may not reflect the full picture. For example, [all-or-nothing thinking]([[All-or-nothing thinking]]) leads us to see situations as completely good or completely bad, with no middle ground. Simply becoming aware of these patterns can be a meaningful first step toward changing them.
- A second strategy many people find useful is gently challenging the thoughts that cause distress. This doesn't mean forcing yourself to think positively or dismissing difficult emotions. Instead, it involves asking honest questions: Is there another way to look at this? What evidence do I have for and against this thought? What might I say to a friend who was thinking this way? Over time, this kind of gentle questioning can help soften the grip of harsh self-judgments or catastrophic thinking.
- Another helpful practice is working toward what therapists sometimes call a "balanced thought" — not an unrealistically positive one, but a more accurate and compassionate one. For instance, shifting from "I always mess everything up" to "I made a mistake this time, and I can learn from it" is a small but meaningful change in how we relate to ourselves.
- Learning to reframe thoughts takes practice, and it isn't always easy to do alone. Many people find it valuable to explore these skills with a therapist or counselor who can offer guidance and support along the way. Reaching out for that kind of help is a sign of strength, not weakness.
