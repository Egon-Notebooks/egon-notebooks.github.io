- Narrative therapy is an approach to mental health support that invites people to look at their lives as stories — ones that can be examined, reframed, and even rewritten. Rather than viewing problems as fixed parts of who someone is, narrative therapy helps people separate themselves from their difficulties and explore new ways of understanding their experiences.
- One of the core ideas in narrative therapy is the practice of "externalizing" a problem. This means treating a challenge — such as [anxiety]([[Understanding anxiety]]), [grief]([[Understanding grief]]), or low self-worth — as something a person experiences rather than something they are. For example, instead of thinking "I am anxious," someone might explore "I am a person who is dealing with anxiety." This subtle but powerful shift can create a sense of distance and agency, making it easier to reflect on how the problem affects life and where it may have come from.
- Another helpful practice involves identifying "unique outcomes" — moments when the problem did not have full control. Many people find that looking back for times they coped well, acted against a pattern, or felt differently can reveal hidden strengths and values they might not have recognized in themselves. These moments can become the foundation for a new, more empowering personal narrative.
- Narrative therapy also encourages people to consider how outside influences — culture, family expectations, societal messages — may have shaped the stories they tell about themselves. Recognizing these external influences can help loosen the grip of unhelpful beliefs that were never truly one's own to begin with.
- It is completely natural to feel uncertain about how to begin reshaping a personal story, especially when struggling. Many people find that working with a trained therapist makes this process feel safer and more meaningful. With the right support, it is possible to move toward a story that reflects your full humanity — not just your hardest moments.
