- All-or-nothing thinking — sometimes called black-and-white thinking — is a common thought pattern where situations, people, or even ourselves get sorted into rigid extremes. Things are either perfect or a total failure, completely good or entirely bad, with little room for anything in between. Many people experience this kind of thinking at times, and it can be especially common during periods of [stress]([[Understanding stress]]), [low mood]([[Understanding low mood]]), or [anxiety]([[Understanding anxiety]]).
- One of the most helpful first steps is simply learning to notice when all-or-nothing thinking is happening. When a small mistake leads to thoughts like "I always mess everything up" or "nothing ever goes right for me," that's often a sign this pattern is at work. Recognizing the thought as a pattern — rather than a fact — can create a small but meaningful shift in perspective.
- From there, many people find it useful to gently challenge the extremes. Asking questions like "Is this really always true?" or "Is there any part of this situation that went okay?" can help introduce more nuance. This isn't about forcing false positivity — it's about making room for the more complicated, realistic picture that most situations actually involve. Keeping a journal to track these moments can help some people notice patterns over time and practice responding to them differently.
- It also helps to practice [self-compassion]([[Self-compassion]]) when this thinking shows up. All-or-nothing thinking often turns inward, leading to harsh self-judgment. Treating yourself with the same understanding you might offer a friend can soften some of that intensity.
- This kind of thinking pattern is something many people work on, and with practice and support it can shift. If all-or-nothing thinking is affecting your daily life or emotional wellbeing, speaking with a mental health professional can be a meaningful step toward building a more balanced inner world.
