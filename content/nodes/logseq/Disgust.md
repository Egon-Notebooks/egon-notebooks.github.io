- Disgust is one of the most universal human emotions — yet it's often overlooked in conversations about mental health. While we tend to focus on feelings like [sadness]([[Sadness]]) or [anxiety]([[Understanding anxiety]]), disgust plays a significant role in how we relate to ourselves, others, and the world around us. Understanding this emotion more fully can open doors to greater self-awareness and emotional wellbeing.
- One important area where disgust shows up is in self-directed feelings. Many people experiencing [shame]([[Shame and guilt]]) or low [self-esteem]([[Self-esteem and where it comes from]]) describe a sense of inner disgust — a feeling of being fundamentally flawed or unacceptable. Research suggests that this kind of self-directed disgust can be linked to conditions such as depression and [body image]([[Body image and self-acceptance]]) difficulties. Recognizing that this feeling is an emotion, not a fact about who you are, is an important first step. Emotions, including disgust, are temporary states — they pass, and they can be worked with.
- Practicing [self-compassion]([[Self-compassion]]) is one approach that many people find helpful when navigating self-directed disgust. This involves treating yourself with the same kindness you might offer a close friend — acknowledging your pain without judgment. Mindfulness-based practices can also support this process, helping people observe difficult emotions with curiosity rather than avoidance. Over time, sitting with an emotion rather than pushing it away can reduce its intensity and power.
- It is also worth exploring where feelings of disgust come from. Cultural messages, past experiences, and deeply held beliefs can all shape what triggers this emotion. Gently examining these influences — ideally with the support of a mental health professional — can help untangle responses that may no longer serve you.
- If disgust, particularly toward yourself, is something you recognize in your own experience, you are not alone, and support is available. Many people find that working through these feelings with a trained professional can lead to meaningful and lasting change.
