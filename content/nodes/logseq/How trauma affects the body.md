- [Trauma]([[Understanding trauma]]) is not only an emotional experience — it also leaves a profound imprint on the body. When a person goes through something overwhelming or threatening, the body's [stress response]([[The stress response]]) activates to help them survive. Long after the event has passed, that response can remain switched on, shaping how someone feels physically day to day.
- The nervous system plays a central role in this process. During a traumatic experience, the body releases [stress]([[Understanding stress]]) hormones like cortisol and adrenaline, preparing a person to fight, flee, or freeze. For some people, this heightened state of alertness does not fully settle afterward. This can show up as physical tension, disrupted sleep, fatigue, digestive issues, or a persistent sense of being "on edge." These are not signs of weakness — they are the body doing what it learned to do to stay safe.
- There are several approaches that many people find helpful in reconnecting with their bodies after trauma. Gentle movement, such as yoga or walking, may help the nervous system gradually shift out of a state of high alert. Mindfulness and breathing practices can support a sense of grounding and safety in the present moment. Somatic therapies — a type of treatment that focuses on the connection between the body and mind — are increasingly recognized as a valuable part of trauma recovery. Working with a trained professional can help a person explore these approaches in a way that feels manageable and safe.
- Understanding that trauma can live in the body is an important step toward healing. Many people find that with time, support, and the right care, their nervous system can begin to feel safer and more settled. Recovery looks different for everyone, and reaching out for professional support is a meaningful and courageous act.
