- Cognitive distortions are patterns of thinking that can quietly shape the way we see ourselves, others, and the world around us. They are often automatic — meaning they arise without us consciously choosing them — and they tend to feel completely true in the moment, even when they don't accurately reflect reality. Learning to recognize these patterns is a meaningful first step toward shifting them.
- One of the most common distortions is "[all-or-nothing thinking]([[All-or-nothing thinking]])," where situations are seen in absolute terms — something is either perfect or a total failure, with no middle ground. For example, making one mistake at work might feel like proof that everything is going wrong. Many people find that gently noticing this pattern and asking themselves "Is there anything in between these two extremes?" can open up a more balanced perspective.
- Another frequent pattern is "[catastrophizing]([[Catastrophizing]])," which involves imagining the worst possible outcome and treating it as though it is likely or inevitable. This can fuel significant [anxiety]([[Understanding anxiety]]) and make ordinary challenges feel overwhelming. Some strategies that may help include grounding techniques — such as focusing on what is known and certain in the present moment — and asking "What is the most realistic outcome here?" rather than the worst-case one.
- "[[Mind reading]]" is also very common — assuming we know what others are thinking, usually negatively, without any real evidence. This can strain relationships and reinforce feelings of self-doubt. Many people find it helpful to notice when they are filling in gaps with assumptions and to consider alternative explanations for another person's behavior.
- It is worth remembering that experiencing cognitive distortions does not mean something is permanently wrong with the way you think. These are patterns many people share, and with time and support, they can change. Speaking with a mental health professional can be a valuable way to explore these thought patterns in a safe, supportive environment.
