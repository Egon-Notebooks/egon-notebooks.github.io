- Panic attacks can feel overwhelming and frightening, but they are a recognized experience that many people go through — and one that can be understood and managed with the right support and tools.
- A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense [fear]([[Fear]]) or discomfort that typically peaks within minutes. Physical sensations such as a racing heart, shortness of breath, dizziness, or chest tightness are common. These feelings are very real, but they are not dangerous. Understanding this distinction — that the body is responding to a perceived threat even when no actual danger is present — can be a meaningful first step toward feeling less afraid of the experience itself.
- One approach many people find helpful is controlled breathing. When panic sets in, the breath often becomes shallow and rapid, which can intensify symptoms. Slowing the breath down — breathing in gently for a few counts, holding briefly, and exhaling slowly — may help signal to the nervous system that it is safe to settle. Grounding techniques are another option some find useful, such as focusing on five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, and so on. This kind of sensory attention can help anchor a person in the present moment when panic feels all-consuming. Over time, working with a mental health professional to understand the patterns and triggers behind panic can also make a significant difference for many people.
- It is worth remembering that experiencing panic attacks does not mean something is permanently wrong. Many people learn to navigate these moments with greater confidence and find that the frequency and intensity of attacks can decrease over time. Reaching out to a healthcare provider or mental health professional is a positive step — support is available, and things can change.
