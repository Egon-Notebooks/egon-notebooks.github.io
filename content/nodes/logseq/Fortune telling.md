- Fortune telling, in the psychological sense, refers to a common thinking pattern where a person assumes they already know how a future event will turn out — usually negatively. This mental habit is sometimes called a cognitive distortion, which simply means a thought pattern that can skew how we interpret the world. Recognizing this tendency is a meaningful first step toward shifting how we experience uncertainty and [anxiety]([[Understanding anxiety]]).
- One strategy many people find helpful is learning to notice when fortune telling is happening. This might look like thoughts such as "I know the interview will go badly" or "There's no point trying because I'll fail." When these thoughts arise, gently questioning them can make a difference. Asking "What evidence do I actually have for this outcome?" or "Have I been wrong about predictions like this before?" can help loosen the grip of a seemingly certain negative conclusion.
- Another approach that many people find useful is practicing sitting with uncertainty rather than resolving it by assuming the worst. This can feel uncomfortable at first, but uncertainty is a natural part of life — and it genuinely means that outcomes are open, not fixed. Mindfulness exercises, such as briefly focusing on the present moment rather than a projected future, may help create a little space between a thought and the weight we give it.
- It can also help to reflect on past situations where feared outcomes did not materialize, or where things turned out differently than expected. Over time, building a small mental record of these moments can gently challenge the brain's tendency to default to worst-case thinking.
- If fortune telling feels persistent or is significantly affecting daily life — relationships, work, or general wellbeing — it may be worth speaking with a mental health professional. Support is available, and thinking patterns, however long-standing, can change with the right guidance.
