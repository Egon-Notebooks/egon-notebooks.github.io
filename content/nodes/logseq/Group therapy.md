- Group therapy is a form of counseling where a small number of people meet together, guided by a trained therapist, to explore shared experiences and support one another. For many people, the idea of opening up in front of others can feel daunting at first — but group therapy has a long history of helping people feel less alone and more understood.
- One of the most meaningful aspects of group therapy is the sense of connection it can create. Hearing others describe feelings or challenges that mirror your own can be deeply validating. Many people find that realizing others share similar struggles helps reduce feelings of [shame]([[Shame and guilt]]) or isolation, which are often among the heaviest burdens of mental health difficulties.
- Group therapy also offers a unique opportunity to practice real-world skills in a safe, supported environment. Depending on the type of group, participants may work on communication, emotional regulation, or building healthier thought patterns — often drawing on approaches like [cognitive behavioral therapy]([[Cognitive behavioral therapy]]) (CBT), which focuses on the relationship between thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. Receiving gentle feedback from peers, not just a therapist, can offer fresh perspectives that individual therapy sometimes cannot.
- Another benefit is consistency and structure. Regular group sessions can provide a reliable rhythm that many people find grounding, especially during periods of uncertainty or change. Being part of a group also creates a gentle sense of accountability — showing up for others can, over time, become a way of showing up for yourself.
- It is completely normal to feel uncertain about whether group therapy is the right fit. Different formats work for different people, and many individuals benefit from a combination of group and individual support. Speaking with a mental health professional can help clarify what options might suit your needs best. Reaching out is a meaningful first step, and support is available.
