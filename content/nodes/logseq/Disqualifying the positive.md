- Disqualifying the positive is a common thinking pattern in which a person brushes aside good experiences, compliments, or achievements as though they don't count. Someone might receive genuine praise at work and immediately think, "They're just being nice," or finish a difficult task and tell themselves, "Anyone could have done that." This pattern can quietly chip away at [self-esteem]([[Self-esteem and where it comes from]]) and make it harder to feel satisfied or hopeful, even when things are genuinely going well.
- One reason this thinking pattern can be difficult to notice is that it often feels like honesty or humility rather than a distortion. A helpful first step is simply becoming aware of when it happens. Many people find it useful to pause after dismissing something positive and ask themselves: "Would I say this about a friend in the same situation?" This small shift in perspective can create enough distance to recognize when self-criticism is operating on an unfair double standard.
- Another strategy that many people find supportive is keeping a brief record of positive events or moments throughout the day — not to force positivity, but to build a more balanced picture of experience over time. This kind of practice, rooted in cognitive behavioral approaches, gently trains attention to notice what is going well alongside what is difficult. Even jotting down one or two small things can begin to loosen the grip of habitual dismissal.
- It is worth remembering that thought patterns, however entrenched they may feel, are not fixed. They developed over time, and with patience and the right support, they can shift. If disqualifying the positive feels persistent or is affecting daily life and wellbeing, speaking with a mental health professional can be a meaningful and effective next step. Many people have found that this kind of support makes a real difference.
