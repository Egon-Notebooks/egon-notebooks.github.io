- Self-esteem is the way we perceive and value ourselves — the ongoing, internal sense of whether we believe we are worthy, capable, and deserving of care. It shapes how we move through relationships, work, and everyday challenges. For many people, understanding where self-esteem comes from is a helpful first step toward nurturing a healthier relationship with themselves.
- Much of our early self-esteem develops through the messages we receive as children — from caregivers, teachers, peers, and the broader culture around us. When those messages were consistently critical, dismissive, or conditional, many people grow into adulthood carrying beliefs about themselves that feel deeply true but were never actually accurate. Recognizing that self-esteem is learned — not fixed or innate — is important, because it means it can also be unlearned and rebuilt over time.
- Life experiences continue to shape self-esteem throughout adulthood. Difficult events like job loss, relationship breakdowns, or repeated criticism can erode a person's sense of worth. At the same time, there are practices that many people find genuinely supportive. Noticing and gently challenging self-critical thoughts — asking whether you would speak to a friend the way you speak to yourself — can gradually shift ingrained patterns. Setting and meeting small, meaningful goals can also help rebuild a sense of competence and trust in oneself. Some people find that practices like journaling or mindfulness help them become more aware of negative self-talk without being overwhelmed by it.
- It is worth remembering that low self-esteem is something many people experience at different points in their lives, and it is not a reflection of personal failure or permanent truth. Support is available, and working with a mental health professional can offer valuable tools for exploring the roots of self-worth and building a more compassionate view of oneself.
