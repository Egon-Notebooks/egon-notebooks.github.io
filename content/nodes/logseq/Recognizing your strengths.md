- Focusing on what we do well can feel unfamiliar, especially during difficult times. Yet recognizing personal strengths is more than a feel-good exercise — it is a practice rooted in psychological research that can support [resilience]([[Building resilience]]), [self-esteem]([[Self-esteem and where it comes from]]), and overall wellbeing. Learning to identify and lean into what makes you capable is a skill that can be developed, even when it doesn't come naturally.
- One approach many people find helpful is reflecting on moments when things went well. Rather than waiting for a grand achievement, consider smaller, everyday examples — a time you stayed patient under pressure, helped a friend navigate a problem, or pushed through a task that felt hard. These moments often point toward strengths that go unnoticed precisely because they feel ordinary to us. Keeping a simple journal of these observations over a few weeks can make patterns easier to spot.
- Another useful strategy involves asking trusted people in your life what they appreciate about you. It can feel vulnerable to ask, but others often notice qualities we overlook in ourselves — reliability, creativity, humor, or a calm presence in difficult situations. Their perspective can offer a more balanced picture than the one our [inner critic]([[The inner critic]]) tends to provide.
- It also helps to broaden how you think about strengths. They are not only talents or skills; they can include values you live by, ways you care for others, or how you handle adversity. Recognizing that perseverance, honesty, or empathy are genuine strengths can shift how you relate to your own story.
- Building awareness of your strengths is not about ignoring challenges or pretending everything is easy. It is about developing a fuller, more compassionate picture of who you are. If this feels difficult to do alone, speaking with a mental health professional can be a meaningful and supportive place to start.
