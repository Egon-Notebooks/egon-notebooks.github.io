- Putting a name to an experience can feel grounding — it can help make sense of something that felt confusing or overwhelming. But the words we use to describe ourselves and our inner lives carry real weight, and sometimes the labels we reach for can do more harm than good.
- Labeling, as explored in [cognitive behavioral therapy]([[Cognitive behavioral therapy]]) (CBT), refers to the habit of attaching fixed, global descriptions to ourselves or others based on a single event or feeling. For example, making a mistake at work and thinking "I am a failure" rather than "I made a mistake" collapses a complex human experience into a single, rigid identity. This kind of thinking pattern — sometimes called mislabeling — often involves harsh or exaggerated language that goes beyond what the situation actually reflects.
- One strategy many people find helpful is learning to separate behaviors and feelings from identity. Instead of "I am lazy," trying "I've been struggling to find motivation lately" keeps the door open for change. The first framing feels permanent; the second acknowledges a state that can shift. Practicing this kind of language, even quietly in one's own thoughts, can gradually soften [the inner critic]([[The inner critic]]).
- Another approach involves noticing the emotional charge behind a label. When a word like "worthless" or "broken" comes to mind, pausing to ask "is this a fact, or is this how I feel right now?" can create a small but meaningful space between thought and belief. Feelings are real and valid — but they are not always accurate summaries of who we are.
- Language shapes the stories we tell about ourselves, and those stories can change. If you find that certain thought patterns are persistent or deeply distressing, speaking with a mental health professional can offer support and practical tools for working through them. Reaching out is a strength, not a sign of weakness.
