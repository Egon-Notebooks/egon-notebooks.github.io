- "Should" is a small word that can carry a surprisingly heavy weight. Many people move through their days with an invisible list of expectations — things they *should* feel, *should* have accomplished, or *should* be by now. While some personal standards are healthy and motivating, rigid "should" thinking can quietly fuel feelings of guilt, [shame]([[Shame and guilt]]), and inadequacy.
- This pattern is recognized in [cognitive behavioral therapy]([[Cognitive behavioral therapy]]) (CBT) — a widely used, evidence-based approach to mental health — as a type of unhelpful thinking style. When we hold ourselves to inflexible rules, we tend to judge our natural human experiences as failures rather than simply as experiences. The same applies when we direct "should" thinking toward others, which can strain relationships and breed resentment.
- One strategy many people find helpful is noticing when "should" language appears in their internal dialogue. Simply pausing to observe a thought — without immediately accepting it as truth — can create a small but meaningful moment of distance. From there, it may be possible to ask: is this expectation realistic? Is it kind? Where did it come from?
- Another approach involves gently reframing the language itself. Replacing "I should exercise more" with "I would like to move my body more, and I can try small steps" shifts the tone from judgment to intention. This isn't about lowering standards — it's about speaking to yourself the way you might speak to someone you care about.
- Many people also find that writing down their "should" thoughts, and then examining them on paper, helps bring perspective that's harder to access inside the mind alone.
- If "should" thinking feels persistent or is connected to deeper feelings of unworthiness or [low mood]([[Understanding low mood]]), reaching out to a mental health professional can make a real difference. Support is available, and these patterns — however familiar they feel — can change.
